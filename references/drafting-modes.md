# Drafting modes

Two modes. Pick by what is being produced, not by who asked. Name the mode in one word before the draft when there is any chance of confusion. Mixing them is the most common correction users make, so get it right the first time.

## Documents mode

For anything that goes into a file and will be read as a record: resume bullets, resume summary, LinkedIn experience entries, LinkedIn headline, skills lists, slide text, a brag sheet.

- Flat and factual. Every line states what was done and what it produced.
- No adjectives about the person. Not "results-driven," "passionate," "proven," "dynamic," "seasoned," "strategic," "innovative," "experienced." The reader decides those.
- Never self-deprecating. No "attempted," no "helped out," no softeners.
- Every bullet ties an action to a measurable outcome where the source has one. Shape: what you did, to what, with what result.
- Short lines. One to two lines per bullet. Fragments are fine here.
- Verbs come from the role pack's Vocabulary section. Use the "use" list and avoid the "avoid" list for the user's role.
- No keyword stuffing. A keyword appears where it is earned.

## Correspondence mode

For anything addressed to a person: email, LinkedIn messages, recruiter replies, thank-you notes, nudges, cover letters, the LinkedIn About section.

- Casual, peer to peer, warm. Reads like someone comfortable in their career with nothing to prove. Even a first-year candidate writes this way; the register is confidence, not seniority.
- Full sentences. Keep the greeting ("Hi Jordan,"). Stacked fragments read clipped in a short note.
- React to the specific thing the person wrote or said before anything else.
- Thank people for specific things. "Thanks for walking me through how the team splits on-call" is a thank-you. "Thanks for your time" is not, unless the next clause names what the time covered.
- Say plainly when something interests you. "The migration work is the part I want to do" beats any amount of enthusiasm language.
- Close warmly. "Looking forward to it" and "Hope the launch goes well" are fine. One line, in your own words.
- Enthusiasm is good when attached to something concrete about the work. Cut generic enthusiasm: "excited for this opportunity," "passionate about," "thrilled," "would love the chance to."
- Short is the default. Match the sender's effort, not their length. A three-line message gets three lines back.

## Both modes

- No em dashes. Use a comma, a period, or a colon.
- No corporate phrasing: "leverage," "synergy," "spearheaded," "circle back" (in writing), "touch base," "reach out" as a noun, "stakeholders" standing alone, "value-add."
- No AI-sounding openers: "I hope this message finds you well," "I wanted to take a moment," "I am writing to," "As a," "Thank you for considering." A plain "I hope you are doing well" is allowed in cold outreach because it is what people write.
- No writer's devices: no "the thing that stuck with me," no "I keep thinking about," no rhetorical questions, no callbacks engineered to look clever.
- Never self-deprecating.
- Never the grateful-candidate posture. The user is a peer who may join this team, not an applicant hoping to be picked.
- Facts about the user come only from the sources in `truthfulness.md`.
