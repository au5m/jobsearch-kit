# Pack: tech

Fields covered: software and technology companies, including security, infrastructure, data, developer tools and SaaS.
Role families: default, sales engineer, software engineer, product manager

The core reads the `### role: default` block of each section, then the block that matches `profile.role_family`, and no other. Vocabulary replaces; every other section adds. Names in examples are placeholders.

## Vocabulary

### role: default

Use: built, shipped, led, ran, designed, migrated, reduced, cut, increased, launched, automated, resolved, scaled, owned (only when the source says so), supported (when the source says so).

Avoid: leveraged, spearheaded, synergized, utilized, facilitated, responsible for, helped with, assisted in, tasked with, participated in, worked on, was involved in.

### role: sales engineer

Use: covered (a territory or set of accounts), supported (a set of account executives), ran the proof of value, ran the proof of concept, won the technical evaluation, technical win, displaced (a competitor) in evaluation, demoed, scoped, built the demo environment, ran the workshop, answered the RFP, enabled (partners or reps), served as the technical lead on.

Avoid: sold, closed, booked, carried a quota, hit quota, generated revenue, drove revenue (these are account-executive verbs and read as the wrong role), owned the deal, negotiated the contract.

The role is pre-sales: the sales engineer wins the technical decision and the account executive closes the commercial one. Every bullet names the technical activity and ties it to a business outcome (evaluation won, deal advanced, competitor displaced, time to value cut).

### role: software engineer

Use: designed, implemented, shipped, refactored, migrated, cut latency, reduced p99, cut build time, raised test coverage, decomposed, instrumented, on-call for, reviewed, mentored, wrote the RFC, led the design review.

Avoid: coded (as a standalone verb), programmed, worked on the codebase, fixed bugs (without saying which or how many), was part of the team that, helped build.

Every bullet names the system, the change, and the measured effect (latency, throughput, cost, reliability, developer time, incidents).

### role: product manager

Use: defined, prioritized, shipped (a feature or launch), owned the roadmap for, ran discovery, wrote the PRD, aligned (named functions) on, launched to (a segment or market), grew (a metric), cut (a metric), decided, traded off.

Avoid: managed stakeholders (standalone), facilitated, collaborated with (standalone), responsible for the product, drove alignment, was the voice of the customer.

Every bullet names the decision or the launch and the metric it moved (adoption, retention, revenue, time to value, support volume).

## Keyword families

### role: default

Group a tech posting's terms into: the core activity, the stack or product categories named, the scale (users, requests, accounts, revenue, team size), the customer or segment (enterprise, mid-market, consumer, developer), methods and frameworks named, and credentials or clearances. Match the posting's own word where the resume shows the same work.

### role: sales engineer

Add: product categories the company sells into and against (name them as the posting does), evaluation activities (proof of value, proof of concept, demo, workshop, RFP, technical win), segment (enterprise, strategic, commercial, mid-market), named competitors, partner and channel work, and any executive-briefing or advisory motion the posting names.

### role: software engineer

Add: languages, frameworks, cloud platforms, data stores, infrastructure and build tooling, system types (distributed, real-time, batch, mobile, embedded), reliability practice (on-call, SLOs, incident response), and scale numbers.

### role: product manager

Add: product surface (platform, API, consumer app, internal tool), customer segment, growth versus core versus platform, metrics named (activation, retention, ARR, NPS), methods (discovery, experimentation, pricing), and the functions the PM aligns (engineering, design, data, go-to-market).

## Ask patterns

### role: default

After a conversation: meet another person on the team, learn about the next stage, or an intro to someone who can speak to the day-to-day. In outreach: a 20 or 30 minute call.

### role: sales engineer

After a hiring-manager conversation, the natural next step is meeting the account executive on the territory, another sales engineer on the team, or the next round. Ask for one of those.

### role: software engineer

After a hiring-manager conversation: meeting an engineer on the team you would join, or the next round. After a peer conversation: nothing to ask; a warm close.

### role: product manager

After a hiring-manager conversation: meeting the engineering or design lead you would partner with, or the next round.

## Reviewer notes

### role: default

A busy reader in tech skims on a phone. They notice whether the note is easy to answer, whether it recaps what they said, and whether it asks for one clear thing. Buzzwords and generic enthusiasm read as filler.

### role: sales engineer

Sales leaders notice whether the candidate writes like a peer who runs evaluations or like an applicant. Any account-executive verb (sold, closed) reads as the wrong role. A recap that names the territory setup or the evaluation motion they described lands; a recap of the candidate's own background does not.

### role: software engineer

Engineering managers and senior engineers notice precision. A recap that names the system or trade-off they described lands. Enthusiasm about "the mission" does not. Over-formal register reads as a mismatch for most engineering teams.

### role: product manager

Product leaders notice whether the candidate reflected the problem or the customer they described, and whether the ask is clear. A note that restates the candidate's framework instead of their conversation reads as pitching.

## Role lens

### role: default

Weigh: whether the work is the kind you want to do, who you would report to and how long they have been there, how the team grew or shrank in the last 18 months, what success looks like in year one, and the company's stability over your horizon.

Questions to validate before joining: what does success look like at six and twelve months; why is the role open; who was in it before and where did they go; how are decisions made; what is the review and promotion rhythm.

### role: sales engineer

Weigh, as a candidate who wins technical evaluations:

- Winnable in an evaluation? Or outgunned in every proof of value? For a pre-launch company, say when an evaluation becomes possible at all.
- Go-to-market leadership quality. Have the founders or the sales leader won enterprise deals in this domain? How long has the sales-engineering leader been in place?
- Category heat. Selling into urgency, or educating skeptics? For which buyers?
- Fit. A short take on how the candidate's background maps to the stack and the buyer. For requirement-by-requirement gap analysis, hand off to the resume skill.

Questions to validate before joining, adjusted to stage:
- Win rate against the top two or three competitors and why they win in evaluations (pre-launch: design-partner conversion instead).
- The sales-engineering org: headcount, ratio of engineers to account executives, who owns demo and lab environments, what enablement exists.
- Quota realism for the product's stage (pre-launch: how year-one quota is set).
- Burn, runway and timing of the next raise.
- Equity, matched to the funding instrument (the core adds the instrument questions).

### role: software engineer

Weigh, as a candidate who will build and run the system:

- The engineering culture in practice: code review, on-call load, incident rate, deploy frequency, how much time goes to legacy versus new work.
- Who leads engineering and for how long; attrition on the team you would join.
- Whether the technical problems match what you want to work on for two or three years.
- Compensation structure and level mapping, since titles vary.

Questions to validate before joining: what does on-call look like per week; what is the deploy process; what broke last quarter and what changed; how is engineering work prioritized against product asks; what does the level ladder look like and where would you land; how is equity granted and refreshed.

### role: product manager

Weigh, as a candidate who will own outcomes without authority:

- Who decides product direction in practice (founder, product, sales, engineering) and how much room a PM has.
- Whether the PM function reports to product, engineering, or a founder, and how long that leader has been there.
- The ratio of PMs to engineers and designers; whether the role is strategy, execution, or both.
- Whether the metrics the company cares about are ones a PM can move.

Questions to validate before joining: what was the last product decision that went against a founder's instinct and how was it made; how is the roadmap set and how often does it change; what does the PM own end to end; how is PM performance measured; who are the engineering and design partners and how long have they been there.

## Interview formats

### role: default

Recruiter screen (fit and logistics), hiring-manager conversation (motivation and depth), peer panel (how you work with others), take-home or work sample (how you produce), presentation of past work (how you communicate), senior-leader final (judgment and trajectory).

### role: sales engineer

- Technical assessment with a live demonstration: stand up an environment, show an outcome, present it. Tests presentation and framing more than infrastructure.
- Hands-on product or API exercise: do something real against the company's product or API. Tests precision and reading documentation.
- Mock discovery call or mock presentation: a sales scenario with the panel as the customer. Tests discovery, positioning, and landing a next step. If the candidate cannot demo the company's product, present a product they know deeply and make the structure the point: discovery, pain, proof, business case, mutual plan.
- Panel with account executives and sales engineers: tests whether the candidate will make the reps' lives easier.

### role: software engineer

- Coding interview (live or take-home): tests problem decomposition and code quality under time.
- System design: tests trade-off reasoning at scale; be ready to go one level down on every box you draw.
- Code review or debugging exercise: tests judgment on someone else's code.
- Behavioral and project deep-dive: tests whether you were hands-on; lead with the specific decision, config or number.

### role: product manager

- Product sense or product critique: tests how you reason about users and trade-offs on an unfamiliar product.
- Case study or take-home: a strategy or prioritization problem; tests structure and the quality of the questions you ask.
- Execution or metrics interview: tests whether you can diagnose a metric change and decide.
- Cross-functional panel with engineering and design: tests how you partner and how you handle disagreement.
- Presentation of a past launch: tests how you tell the story of a decision and its result.

## Examples

### role: default

Before: Worked on the migration of the reporting system to the cloud.
After: Migrated the reporting system to [cloud platform] for [# of] internal users, cutting monthly infrastructure cost by [%] and report latency from [minutes] to [seconds].

### role: sales engineer

Before: Responsible for technical sales activities for enterprise accounts in the region.
After: Ran technical evaluations for [#] enterprise accounts across [region], winning [X of Y] proofs of value against [competitor category].

Before: Helped customers with deployments of the platform.
After, only if the source says the candidate led them: Led proof-of-value deployments for [#] prospects in [segment].
If the source only says "helped," keep "Supported."

### role: software engineer

Before: Worked on backend services for the payments team.
After: Decomposed the payments monolith into [#] services on [platform], cutting p99 checkout latency from [ms] to [ms] and deploy time from [hours] to [minutes].

### role: product manager

Before: Managed the roadmap for the onboarding product.
After: Owned onboarding for [segment], shipping [#] releases in [period] that raised week-one activation from [%] to [%] and cut setup-related support tickets by [%].
